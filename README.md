# Custom-packaging-solutions
Custom packaging solutions: what to specify, what to ask, and what goes wrong
Start with the product, not the box 
 <a href="https://jchtrade.com/packaging" rel="nofollow">Custom packaging solutions</a>
A box is designed around a physical object, so the first package of information is the product itself:

A real sample, or the final CAD. Dimensions off a spec sheet miss cord bundles, hang tags, the battery pack that ships loose and the instruction booklet that adds a few millimeters.
Everything that goes in the pack. Product, accessories, manual, warranty card, desiccant, spare parts. Each one needs a place.
Weight and fragility. A glass jar, a die-cast part and a soft bag need very different structures.
How it is sold. On a shelf, on a peg hook, in a club store tray, or straight to a customer's door. A retail box is a billboard; an e-commerce mailer is a shipping container first.
If the product is still changing, say so. A box sized to a pre-production sample is the most common reason a pack has to be re-cut after the first production lot.

Structure and board: what the factory needs to know
Every printed pack starts as a dieline: the flat drawing of the box with its cuts, folds and glue areas. "Dielines can also indicate perforation, cutting, and folding marks for flat packaging." "A dieline is usually not printed on the final piece but is used to determine correct layout." Ask the factory for its dieline for your size, put your artwork on it, and have them confirm it before anyone prints.

The material falls into three families.

Folding cartons are printed paperboard boxes: the tuck-end box most retail products come in. Board is specified by grade and thickness. In the US thickness is quoted in points, "where a sheet of paperboard with a thickness of 0.024" would be 24 points." They suit light products: "folding cartons are generally limited to holding a few pounds or kilograms of material."
Corrugated is fluted board between liners. It is what shipping cartons are made of, and in finer flutes it also makes printed retail and mailer boxes. "Finer flutes such as E and F flutes provide smoother printing surfaces for high-resolution graphics, making them widely adopted for custom retail packaging and mailer boxes."
Rigid boxes are thick chipboard wrapped in printed paper, often with a magnetic flap or a separate lid. They do not fold flat unless designed to, cost more per unit, and are used for gift and presentation packs.
For corrugated, two numbers do most of the work. "Burst strength is the pressure required to rupture corrugated sheet." The edge crush test (ECT) is the other: "It gives information on the ability of a particular board construction to resist crushing." ECT matters most when cartons are stacked on a pallet or in a container. "A box maker's certificate is typically printed on what will become the underside of the box." Ask what board grade the quote is based on, and check the certificate on the first cartons against it.

Print, finish and color
Print and finish are where custom packaging gets expensive quickly, and where most disputes come from. Put these in writing:

Printed tins, a blister card, a black presentation box with a fitted insert and a purple shipping carton laid out on a conference table for review
Packaging samples laid out for review from a program Jack has worked on: tins, a blister card, a fitted presentation box and the carton they ship in.
Print method and colors. Process color (CMYK), spot colors, or both. If your brand color must match, name the spot color and ask for a printed drawdown or press proof on the actual board. The same ink looks different on white coated board and on brown kraft.
Coatings and laminations. Gloss or matte lamination, soft-touch, varnish, spot UV. Lamination protects the print from scuffing in transit; an uncoated box often arrives scuffed.
Embellishments. Foil stamping, embossing and debossing each need their own tooling and add a step on the line.
Inside print. Printing the inside of a box is a second pass and a cost line. Decide it before the quote, not after.
The usual sequence is the same one Jack runs on every pack: a dieline, a proof, a sample, then the run, checked on the line. Approve a physical sample made from the production board with the production finish before the run. A digital proof shows the artwork is right. It does not show how the foil sits on soft-touch lamination.

Inserts and pack-out
The insert keeps the product still and decides the unboxing. Common choices are paperboard or corrugated inserts, die-cut foam, molded pulp, and formed plastic trays. Each has its own tooling, lead time and look. A thermoformed tray or a clear blister needs a mold; for retail cards and clamshells the details are on our blister and clamshell packaging page.

Write down the pack-out as well: what goes in first, which way the product faces, where the manual sits, whether there is a seal or a sticker. The pack-out sheet is what the line follows and what an inspector checks against. A photo of an approved packed sample, with every part labeled, prevents most pack-out errors.

Shipping, labeling and retail rules
The retail box is only half the system. It travels inside a master carton, on a pallet, inside a container. Specify:

Printed retail boxes for green plates stacked in a corrugated master carton with paperboard dividers, one box open to show the plates inside
Printed retail boxes packed into a corrugated master carton with dividers. The retail box and the shipper are specified together.
Inner and master cartons. How many units per inner, how many per master, the board grade, and the maximum gross weight per carton your warehouse or carrier accepts.
Carton marks. Product, quantity, carton number, gross and net weight and country of origin on the outside. The details are in our note on shipping marks for export cartons.
Barcodes. Most US retailers expect a GS1-issued UPC on the retail pack and their own labels or codes on the master carton. Get the retailer's vendor guide before artwork is final.
Label content. For many consumer goods sold in the US, the Fair Packaging and Labeling Act sets the basics. As Wikipedia summarizes it, "It requires the label to state: The identity of the product; The name and place of business of the manufacturer, packer, or distributor; and The net quantity of contents." Food, drugs, cosmetics, toys and electronics each carry further rules of their own.
